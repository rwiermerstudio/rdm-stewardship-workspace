Meridian's fictional rules

These ten short documents describe the choices Meridian makes for this lesson. They are fictional, not excerpts from a real institute. We have shortened the rules so you can follow who prepares the work, who checks it and why it may come back. Real decisions also need current law, ethics decisions, signed consent records, community agreements and archive terms. No passage grants permission to share or reuse real material. The source catalogue keeps the exact document IDs, versions and archive details.

MI-DOC · Keeping track of changes

Version 2026.1 · fictional institutional policy board · all Meridian exercise projects.

§1 Who prepares the note

The researcher explains which original files they used and what they changed. The data steward is a colleague who helps organise and check research material. They read the note because a colleague should be able to understand the work without asking the researcher to remember every detail.

§2 Keep the original and explain the change

Keep the original files and write down what you changed. Include who did it, when, which copies they used, the method or software version, and where a colleague can find the check. Add a dated correction rather than overwriting the old account. This history helps people understand why a result changed. The steward sends an unclear note back to the researcher. A link to a check is sometimes called an evidence pointer; the example references here are unverified.

MI-PRES · Preparing work for an archive

Version 2026.1 · fictional institutional policy board · all Meridian exercise projects.

§1 Prepare a file list

The researcher lists the files they would send, their versions, types and sizes. Keep related instructions with them so the archive knows what it would receive. Use the invented examples here, not real research files.

§2 Decide about descriptions and files separately

A title or description may reveal a person or place even when the file stays private. Ask who can decide whether the description may be public, and who can decide about using the files. Meridian separates these questions because closing a recording does not hide its catalogue title.

§3 Check that the copies arrive unchanged

Before a real transfer, the researcher and archive agree on a file list and a way to check each copy. A checksum is a calculated value used to detect a changed file. The archive checks these values when files arrive and at agreed intervals afterward. This page does not run those checks or look after files.

§4 Ask the curator before sending files

Give the archive curator the file list, expected size, limits on use and a plan for checking the copies. The curator compares the real files with available space and the archive terms. They may return a list that is incomplete. A service can look suitable on paper yet be unable to take this particular collection, so a shortlist is not acceptance.

MI-PRIV · Confidentiality review

Version 2026.2 · fictional institutional policy board · restricted exercise projects.

§1 Explain the intended use

When material is private or sensitive, the researcher describes who wants to use it and for what purpose. Meridian asks this first because permission for one study may not cover another. A private label alone does not establish permission.

§2 Consider what people could learn

The privacy reviewer considers both the description and the files. Could someone recognise a participant by combining details with information elsewhere? The reviewer needs the proposed use and the actual agreements to answer. An archive that handles access requests cannot answer on their behalf.

§3 Return questions that cannot yet be answered

The researcher sends the proposed purpose, safeguards, relevant promises and missing checks to the privacy reviewer. The reviewer may ask for a narrower purpose or the signed agreement. Record unanswered questions rather than treating a reply as approval. The person responsible for consent, the community body and the ecology specialist keep their own decisions; a privacy review does not replace them.

ASTRO-FITS · Astronomy method record

Version 1.0 · fictional astronomy panel · astronomy exercise project.

§1 Explain how the sky images were corrected

Keep the original image names, the corrected image names, the processing instructions and the calibration inputs used to correct the measurements. Link them to the quality check so another scientist can follow the correction. FITS and Zarr are file formats used in this example. The large archive lists them, but its curator still needs to check the files and available space.

SOC-DDI · Interview description

Version 1.0 · fictional social science panel · social-science exercise project.

§1 Keep the interview context

The researcher keeps the interview guide and explains how the team assigned codes to the interviews. The steward checks that the table refers to the right guide. This helps later readers understand what people were asked without making private interview text public.

§2 Explain the codes and check the proposed use

Keep a guide that defines each code in the table. This is often called a codebook. Check whether its headings or small groups could identify someone before sharing even the guide. The source catalogue names a description standard, but this exercise does not test compliance with it. The privacy reviewer separately checks whether the proposed use fits the interview agreements.

GEN-USE · A new use of genetic material

Version 2.0 · fictional genomics panel · genomics exercise project.

§1 Describe the proposed study

The researcher explains what they want to learn from the genetic material and why they need a new analysis. This gives the person responsible for participant consent a specific request to compare with the agreements.

§2 Keep track of the proposed copies

Name the original sequence files, the proposed files of genetic differences and the software version that would make them. Include a reference to the proposed quality check. The steward can ask for a clearer method note while the analysis remains paused.

§3 Ask about protected storage

The Secure Data Vault may have space for the files and ways to limit who can use them. Those are reasons to consider it for storage. They do not tell the researcher whether participants agreed to this study.

§4 Find the agreement and its owner

Send the proposed purpose and available consent records to the authorized consent owner, the person responsible for deciding what the agreement covers, and the privacy reviewer. If the agreement cannot be found or its scope is unclear, record that gap and keep the work paused.

§5 Wait for the consent decision

Do not start the new analysis or release files while consent and approved purpose remain unknown. Only the authorized consent owner can decide whether this use is covered, with privacy review where required. Meridian chooses a firm stop here because a storage decision cannot repair missing permission. A practice answer or a rewritten note cannot clear the hold.

HER-COMM agreement · Community-governed collection

Version 1.0 · fictional community-appointed circle · community-governed exercise project. This is an invented agreement, not a representation of any real community's protocol.

§1 Let the community decide its questions

The community-appointed circle decides proposed uses and whether people may find the collection under this invented agreement. The researcher prepares a request, and the steward helps make it clear. Meridian gives the circle this role because the institute should not decide for the people whose collection it holds. Real communities have their own agreements and processes.

§2 Ask about the title and recordings separately

Keep the title private while asking the circle whether it may be seen at all. Ask separately whether people may request recordings. A title can reveal a tradition even without the audio. Record the actual community decision outside this exercise before changing either setting. The privacy office must also check the consent terms; it cannot replace the circle.

BIO-DWC · Protecting rare sites

Version 1.0 · fictional ecology panel · biodiversity exercise project.

§1 Explain the map

Keep the original observation record private. Explain how the team replaced exact sites with larger areas, including how large those areas are. A later reader needs to know what the map can and cannot show. Keep exact rare-site locations out of public titles, previews and this exercise.

§2 Get an independent check of location risk

A larger map area may still reveal a rare site when combined with a caption or other clues. Keep the map and its description private until an independent ecology specialist checks that risk outside the exercise. Meridian requires another person because the team making the map may overlook clues familiar to local readers. Neither a broader area nor a funder request clears this hold.

NEU-BIDS · Checking brain scans

Version 1.0 · fictional imaging panel · neuroimaging exercise project.

§1 Identify the scans and changed copies

Name the original scanning session, the changed copies and the method used to remove facial structure. The steward needs this account to work out which scans and instructions belong together.

§2 Keep the processing record

Keep the software version and a reference to the planned image check. A software run can miss identifying details, so a note that it ran does not show that it succeeded. The references made by this page are unverified.

§3 Ask about each planned use

Decide separately who may read the scan descriptions and who may use the images. A record saying participants gave consent is a starting point; the privacy reviewer still needs to check whether it covers this particular use.

§4 Inspect the images and companion information

A trained reviewer checks the scans for remaining facial details. The privacy office checks the small companion files for details that could identify someone and for limits on use. Keep information needed to understand the scans rather than deleting every field. The catalogue names an imaging standard; meeting that standard and completing these checks require the real files outside this page.

FUN-OPEN · Open outputs preference

Version 1.0 · fictional funder officer · open-futures exercise projects.

§1 Help others use the research

The funder prefers descriptions and suitable results to be available for others to use. Sharing can let another team understand or build on the work. This preference does not give the researcher permission to publish private material.

§2 Check the description too

Review the title and description separately from the files. Keeping a map private will not help if its public caption reveals the rare site. Meridian uses separate questions so the smaller description is not forgotten.

§3 Ask who can give permission

The researcher records who owns the relevant rights and what restrictions apply. The responsible person checks this account. Collecting a recording or changing a file does not by itself give the researcher the right to publish it.

§4 Prepare a useful changed copy

Explain how a public copy could omit private details while keeping enough context to be useful. A colleague needs to understand what was removed and why. The proposed copy still needs review before publication.

§5 Explain why work remains private

Promises to participants, confidentiality, community agreements and rare-site review take priority over the wish to publish. Record the reason for waiting so the next person can understand it. Meridian chooses this order to avoid treating a publication deadline as permission.

§6 Publish only after the relevant decisions

Share a description or changed copy only after the people responsible for rights and disclosure have approved that specific output. An unresolved independent ecology review means the rare-site map and description stay private. The funder preference cannot clear a hold or give access.

Candidate repository screening, not acceptance

Meridian lists four possible services. The General Archive handles smaller mixed collections. The Secure Data Vault is intended for private material with controlled access. The Large-Object Archive is intended for very large collections such as sky images. The Community-Governed Collection follows the appointed community body when considering requests. These differences give the researcher a useful shortlist, but all four remain candidate-only. The curator must check the actual file types, size, location requirements, rights, space and copy checks before accepting work. Exact limits remain in the source catalogue; this page does not need you to memorise a list of formats.
